Krishna asks Arjuna to hear how, with the mind attached to him and practising yoga in his refuge, he can be known fully and without doubt.|Bring attention and sincerity to study, rather than treating it as a collection of detached facts.
Krishna will explain knowledge together with realised understanding, after which nothing essential remains to be known about this subject.|Distinguish knowing a definition from living its meaning. Choose one idea to test in conduct.
Among thousands, few strive for perfection; among those who strive, few truly know Krishna.|Value the depth of the undertaking without using difficulty to feel superior to others.
Earth, water, fire, air, space, mind, intellect and ego comprise Krishna’s eightfold lower nature.|Study this traditional account of nature as a philosophical framework, not a modern chemistry classification.
Beyond that lower nature is a higher nature, the living principle through which the world is sustained.|Notice the text’s distinction between material processes and life or consciousness before merging them into one idea.
All beings arise from these natures; Krishna is described as the source and dissolution of the whole universe.|Reflect on dependence and impermanence rather than imagining yourself entirely self-created.
Nothing is higher than Krishna; everything rests on him like jewels strung on a thread.|Use the image to understand the claimed divine unity behind visible variety.
Krishna is the taste in water, light in the sun and moon, Om in the Vedas, sound in space and human capacity.|Let an ordinary experience, such as drinking water, become a moment of gratitude and remembrance.
He is the pure fragrance of earth, the brilliance of fire, life in beings and the discipline of ascetics.|Notice life and ability as gifts to use responsibly, rather than merely possessions to display.
He is the enduring seed of beings, the intelligence of the intelligent and the radiance of the radiant.|When admiring someone’s talent, learn from it without making comparison a source of resentment.
Krishna is strength free from selfish desire and attachment, and desire that does not conflict with dharma.|Ask whether an ambition supports ethical life or demands that you neglect it.
States arising from sattva, rajas and tamas depend on Krishna, yet he is not confined by them.|Observe a mental state without treating it as the whole of reality or your permanent identity.
The world, confused by the three gunas, does not recognise the unchanging divine reality beyond them.|Notice how your current mood colours your interpretation. Recheck a judgment once the mood changes.
Krishna’s maya, consisting of the gunas, is difficult to cross; those who take refuge in him cross it.|Within this devotional path, combine effort with humility about the limits of control.
Those whose understanding is obscured and whose conduct turns toward destructive tendencies do not seek refuge in Krishna.|Use this as a mirror for your own resistance to truth, not a label for everyone outside your tradition.
Four kinds of good-hearted people worship Krishna: the distressed, seekers of understanding, seekers of benefit and the wise.|Identify your present reason for practice honestly. A beginning motive need not be the final one.
Among them, the wise person with steady, undivided devotion is especially dear to Krishna.|Move beyond asking only for relief or reward; make room for understanding and love.
All these devotees are noble, but the wise person is described as Krishna’s very self, established in him as the highest goal.|Respect sincere beginnings while recognising the text’s emphasis on mature devotion.
After many births, the wise take refuge in Krishna, knowing Vasudeva as all; such a great soul is rare.|Think of maturity as deepening understanding and devotion, not a status you can quickly claim.
People led by various desires turn toward other deities and follow practices shaped by their own nature.|Ask how desire shapes what you seek spiritually, including in practices you already know.
Whichever form a devotee sincerely wishes to worship, Krishna makes that faith steady.|Respect the seriousness of another person’s devotion without assuming every interpretation is identical.
With that faith, the person worships and obtains desired benefits, though Krishna is their ultimate source.|Recognise the difference between a received benefit and the wider spiritual purpose of practice.
Such rewards are temporary; worshippers of devas reach them, while Krishna’s devotees reach him.|Consider whether your main aim is a passing reward or a lasting spiritual relationship.
Those without understanding think Krishna’s manifestation exhausts his reality, missing his higher, imperishable nature.|Do not assume that what you can see or describe fully contains what the text calls divine.
Veiled by yoga-maya, Krishna is not recognised by all as unborn and imperishable.|Admit the limits of ordinary perception rather than turning unfamiliarity into automatic rejection or easy certainty.
Krishna knows beings of the past, present and future, while they do not fully know him.|Approach this claim as theology; let it encourage humility rather than claims that you personally know the future.
Beings become confused through the opposing forces of desire and aversion from birth.|Notice a strong preference before treating it as an objective description of the situation.
Those whose wrongdoing has been purified and who move beyond deluding opposites worship Krishna with firm commitment.|Repair conduct and practise steadiness alongside devotional study.
Those seeking freedom from aging and death through refuge in Krishna come to understand Brahman, the self and action.|Keep the chapter’s liberating purpose in view, beyond requests for worldly improvement.
Those who know Krishna in relation to beings, divine principles and sacrifice can remember him with a steady mind even at death.|Cultivate remembrance in ordinary life instead of postponing all reflection until a crisis.
