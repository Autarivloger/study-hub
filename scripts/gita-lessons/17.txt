Arjuna asks about people who worship sincerely but without following scriptural guidance: is their trust sattvic, rajasic or tamasic?|Ask about the quality and effect of a practice, not merely whether someone feels strongly about it.
Krishna says embodied people’s trust is threefold, arising from their nature: sattvic, rajasic and tamasic.|Notice that conviction itself needs examination; intensity of belief does not guarantee clarity.
A person’s trust accords with their inner disposition; what they deeply trust shapes who they become.|Review what repeatedly receives your confidence, time and attention.
Sattvic people worship devas; rajasic people yakshas and rakshasas; tamasic people spirits and ghostly beings. The categories belong to this sacred framework.|Read these traditional associations without treating unfamiliar religious people as objects of ridicule.
Some practise severe, unauthorised austerities, driven by hypocrisy, ego, craving and attachment.|Be wary of discipline that demands admiration or ignores the harm it causes.
Tormenting the body’s elements and the divine presence within, such people follow a destructive resolve.|Do not injure or starve yourself to prove spiritual seriousness. Care for the body supports practice.
Food, sacrifice, discipline and giving are also threefold; Krishna will explain their differences.|Examine ordinary routines as part of spiritual life rather than separating ethics from daily habits.
Foods supporting life, clarity, strength, health, ease and satisfaction, and experienced as wholesome and nourishing, are dear to the sattvic.|Choose balanced, suitable nourishment. The verse is traditional guidance, not a complete medical diet.
Excessively bitter, sour, salty, hot, pungent, dry or burning foods are dear to the rajasic and may bring distress.|Notice excess and your own needs without turning food preference into a moral ranking of people.
Stale, spoiled, putrid or otherwise impure food is dear to the tamasic.|Prioritise safe storage and food hygiene. The verse’s ritual categories are not a substitute for modern food-safety guidance.
Sacrifice offered as a duty, according to sound guidance and without craving for reward, is sattvic.|Perform one worthwhile act because it is right, without needing public recognition.
Sacrifice performed for reward or display is rajasic. Its purpose is a personal payoff or public impression rather than a sincere offering of duty.|Notice when the audience matters more to you than the good the act could do.
Sacrifice without guidance, shared food, sacred recitation, appropriate giving or trust is tamasic.|Consider whether your practice lacks care, understanding or generosity, rather than merely checking its appearance.
Reverence for the divine, teachers and the wise, cleanliness, straightforwardness, restraint and nonviolence are discipline of the body.|Choose a bodily discipline that supports dignity and non-harm rather than punishment.
Speech that does not needlessly disturb, that is truthful, considerate and beneficial, together with study, is discipline of speech.|Before a difficult conversation, make the truth clear, useful and respectful without hiding what needs to be said.
Serenity, gentleness, measured silence, self-guidance and purity of intention are discipline of the mind.|Notice the motive behind your next response, then choose a calmer and more honest one.
These three disciplines, practised with deep trust and without desire for reward, are sattvic.|Practise privately as carefully as publicly. Consistency reveals the intention.
Discipline undertaken to gain honour, respect or admiration is rajasic, unstable and temporary.|Ask whether you would continue a helpful practice if nobody could see it.
Discipline performed through confused stubbornness, self-torment or the wish to harm another is tamasic.|Stop treating pain or humiliation as proof of virtue. Choose a sustainable and non-harming practice.
A gift given because giving is right, at a suitable place and time, to an appropriate recipient, without expecting return, is sattvic.|Offer useful help that respects the recipient’s dignity and actual needs.
A gift given expecting repayment or benefit, or given reluctantly, is rajasic.|Notice hidden conditions in your generosity. Make them explicit or release them where appropriate.
A gift given carelessly at an unsuitable time or place, to an unsuitable purpose, or with contempt is tamasic.|Check that your help is wanted, useful and offered respectfully.
Om, Tat and Sat are described as a threefold designation of Brahman, associated with sacred learning, sacrifice and the Vedas.|Learn what a sacred expression means in context rather than using it as decoration.
Those who follow the Vedic way begin sacrifice, giving and discipline with Om.|If using sacred recitation, let it gather attention and intention rather than become an automatic sound alone.
Seekers of liberation use Tat, “That,” with offering, discipline and giving, relinquishing possessiveness toward reward.|Before a good act, practise releasing the demand that it add to your status.
Sat signifies reality and goodness, and is also used for an auspicious, worthy act.|Connect a valued word with a truthful action rather than relying on the word itself.
Steadiness in sacrifice, discipline and giving, and action serving these, is also called Sat.|Give a helpful practice continuity; a small reliable contribution can matter more than a dramatic gesture.
Whatever is offered, given or practised without trust is called asat and lacks the intended spiritual fruit here or hereafter.|Review whether your practice has become empty performance. Renew understanding before simply adding more.
